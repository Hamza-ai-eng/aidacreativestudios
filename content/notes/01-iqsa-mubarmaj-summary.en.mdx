A system gives you a list of names. Twenty seconds to decide. One error in ten.

This is not a mistake. This is the math.

In 2023, the Israeli government deployed an automated classification system called Lavender — a targeting apparatus that selected individuals in Gaza based on intercepted intelligence, satellite imagery, and phone records. The system reported: this person is a military operative. Confidence level: 90 percent. Known error rate: 10 percent.

Ten percent means one in every ten people the system marked as a target was not a fighter.

The approval came from the government level. It was not debated. The military proposed it. Lawyers said: "The expected error rate aligns with international law." The government approved it.

The strikes began. Families were buried in rubble. Children died. A mother told a journalist: "Nobody here knew anything about the military."

When you read "data error," do not picture a small mistake. Picture a man tortured because of a number in a spreadsheet.

The system spread. A second version — called "Where's Daddy?" — specialized in targeting field commanders. Same logic. Same calculated error. Same result.

The question is not technical. The engineering was sound. The question is that the government chose to say: "This error rate is acceptable." In law, this is called "proportionality" — the calculation between expected harm and military advantage. The government claimed the ratio was balanced.

When you read the full report, you will see the numbers: 37,000 names on the first list. Number of strikes executed. Number of people killed. Among them, the count of those with no military connection.

Here is what is strange: after the war, the military released civilian casualty figures higher than they initially claimed. Which means: the error was not 10 percent.

It was higher.

The strike in 2023 on the building where the military unit was supposedly located? 130 civilians killed. One member of the unit.

This is the real calculation.
